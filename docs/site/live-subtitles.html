HearAble · Italian live subtitles · DVB · on-device

I built live subtitles for a friend's TV

Italian subtitles on live broadcast television, about one second after the voice, with no cloud services. The audio comes from inside the receiver he already had.

By Nunzio Raciti, with Claude as development assistant · source on GitHub

The problem

A close friend of mine is hard of hearing. In Italy many TV programmes have no subtitles at all: news from local channels, sport, talk shows, a big part of the daytime schedule. When subtitles exist, sometimes they arrive so late that they describe a sentence that ended long before. For him, watching TV often means watching people move their lips.

So I asked myself: can I put subtitles on his television, live, in Italian, without cloud services and without changing the way he uses the TV?

What I built

The project is called HearAble. It shows Italian subtitles on live broadcast television, about one second after the voice. It runs on two machines:

  • the satellite/terrestrial receiver he already had under the TV, an Enigma2 box running Linux;
  • a small fanless mini PC, connected to the receiver with one Ethernet cable.

Nothing goes to the internet. There is no account and no subscription. He presses one button on the remote control and the subtitles appear. The mini PC wakes up by itself when he presses the button, and switches itself off a few minutes after the TV goes quiet. It also works with films played from a USB stick on the receiver.

The key idea

For me the most interesting part was the audio. A speech recogniser needs the audio of the programme. A microphone near the TV picks up the noise of the room. An HDMI audio extractor means more hardware and more delay.

But the receiver is already separating the audio from the TV signal, for its own work. Linux has a standard interface to read it, the DVB demux, so I could read the audio track directly with a filter on its packet ID. No extra tuner, no re-encoding, nothing changed in the firmware of the box. And because the signal is read when it enters the receiver, the audio arrives 64 milliseconds before the picture is on the screen.

open("/dev/dvb/adapter0/demux0", O_RDWR | O_NONBLOCK)
DMX_SET_PES_FILTER    pid    = <the audio PID the viewer selected>
                      input  = DMX_IN_FRONTEND
                      output = DMX_OUT_TSDEMUX_TAP
                      flags  = DMX_IMMEDIATE_START
read()                /* 188-byte MPEG-TS packets */

The mini PC receives about 26 kB/s of compressed audio, runs an open speech recognition model (NVIDIA Nemotron, quantised, on the CPU only) at about half of real time, and sends back two lines of text. The receiver draws them at the bottom of the screen.

Some numbers

  • about 1.0 second between the spoken word and the subtitle;
  • real-time factor 0.48 on an Intel N95, with no GPU;
  • 30 minutes of live TV with faults injected on purpose: no lost audio, no old subtitles left on screen;
  • 19 faults injected, 19 automatic recoveries.

Three things that went wrong

1. The cheap board did not make it. I wanted to run everything on a single-board computer of about 50 euros. I measured five different ways to do it. To reach real time it had to be about 7 times faster, and its memory bandwidth allowed less than 3. Physics closed that door. I was not happy, but the measurement saved me weeks of hope.

2. The instrument was wrong, my friend was right. At one point the measurements said that my audio was 2.4 seconds ahead of the picture, so the system was waiting on purpose before showing the subtitles. My friend said that the subtitles felt late. We measured again, this time reading two clocks at the same instant inside the receiver, and the real value was 64 milliseconds. The old measurement was wrong by a factor of forty. Since then, when a person and an instrument disagree, I check the instrument first.

3. A fix that made things worse. Sometimes the last word of a sentence appeared only together with the next sentence. The first fix looked very good in the tests. On the real TV it was worse: about half of the words it released were changed by the model a moment later. I removed it. The solution that works uses a different signal: the word is released when the model writes a full stop. A pause is only a guess. A full stop is the model saying that the sentence is finished. Sentences the model does not punctuate still have the problem.

How it was built

I did not type most of the code. I worked with Claude, an AI model by Anthropic, as a development assistant: it wrote the code, the tests and the measurement tools. I defined the goal and the constraints, I made the design decisions, I took care of the hardware, and I tested everything on the real TV.

  • The AI is fast, but it can be wrong with great confidence. More than once it presented a conclusion as certain, and a real test showed that it was not true. My job was to ask for the measurement, not to accept the explanation.
  • Real use found what the tests did not. Building everything again from zero found three problems that no automated test had seen. Pulling the cable, cutting the power and using the remote control like a normal person found others.

Try it

The code is on GitHub: github.com/nuraci/HearAble. The documentation explains both machines in detail.

I would be very happy if someone tries it on a different receiver, adapts it to another language, or uses it to help a person who cannot follow the TV. The documentation was written while building it, and it was never tested from zero on a new machine, so if something is missing, please open an issue.
